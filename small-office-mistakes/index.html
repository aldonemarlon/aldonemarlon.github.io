Small home offices tend to go wrong in the same five ways. They are all decisions made early, usually in the first week, and they are all reversible — but reversing them costs money you already spent.

Here they are, in the order they usually happen.

1. Buying storage before you know what you are storing

This is the expensive one.

The instinct on day one is to buy organisers. Drawer dividers, desktop caddies, a set of matching boxes. Then over the following weeks the desk fills up with things none of those containers fit, and the organisers themselves are now occupying space.

Instead: work at the desk for two weeks and pay attention to what is still on it at the end of each day. That pile is your requirement. Buy for that pile.

2. Buying the biggest desk that fits

A desk that fills the room turns the room into an office. In a bedroom or a shared space, that is a bad trade you make once and live with daily.

A larger surface also does not stay clear. It fills to whatever size you give it — the desk equivalent of a bigger closet not solving anything.

Instead: buy the smallest desk you can comfortably work at, and put storage underneath and beside it rather than on it. You need roughly ten inches of depth for keyboard and forearms; everything past that is negotiable.

3. Ignoring the floor

Cable mess sits at floor level where nothing competes with it, so your eye reads the whole room as untidy even when the desk is clear. People then try to fix the feeling by tidying the desk, which was not the problem.

Instead: get the power strip and cable slack off the floor before you organise anything on top.

4. Treating lighting as decoration

One ceiling fixture behind you produces glare, screen reflections and your own shadow across the desk. A single desk lamp does not fix it — it often increases the contrast.

Instead: think in layers. Task light on the desk, aimed across rather than down. A second light behind the monitor bouncing off the wall. That second one is the layer almost everyone skips and it is the one that reduces eye strain most.

5. Building a system that needs willpower

The last mistake is structural rather than a purchase. A workspace that requires decisions to reset — where does this go, which drawer was that in — stops being reset within about two weeks.

Instead: design the end-of-day reset to take under a minute and require no decisions. Three actions, fixed homes, done.
