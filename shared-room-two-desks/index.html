Two People, One Room: Sharing a Home Office That Fits

When two people work from home in one small room, the instinct is to buy a second desk and find somewhere to put it. That usually produces a room where both people can work but neither can concentrate.

The constraint is not floor space. It is that two working people in one room generate two sets of sound, two calls, and two sightlines, and the layout is what keeps those from colliding.

Do not put the desks side by side

Side by side feels efficient and is the worst option. You share a sightline, you share a sound field, and every call one person takes is in the other person's ears at the same volume.

Two layouts work better:

Back to back. Desks facing opposite walls, chairs toward each other with a gap between. Nobody is in anybody's screen, and turning around is a deliberate act rather than a constant peripheral presence. This is the best option in most rectangular rooms.

Diagonal corners. Desks in opposite corners, both facing into their corner. Maximum distance in a small room, and each person gets a defined zone.

Whichever you pick, the rule is that neither person should be in the other's field of view while working.

Calls are the real problem

Two people, two calls, one room. There is no layout that fixes this fully — but there is equipment that does most of it.

Two small desks beat one big one

The temptation is a single long desk for two. Avoid it — you get the side-by-side problem with no way to change the layout, and any vibration one person makes travels to the other's screen.
