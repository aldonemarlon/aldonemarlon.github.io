Most rooms that become home offices were never lit for working. They have one fixture in the middle of the ceiling, and that fixture is behind you when you sit at the desk.

That produces three problems at once: your own shadow falls across the desk, the light reflects off the screen, and the screen ends up being the brightest thing in an otherwise dim room. Your eyes spend the whole day adjusting between the two.

Adding one desk lamp does not fix this. It usually makes the contrast worse.

The principle: three sources, not one

Comfortable working light comes from layers, not from brightness. You want:

  1. Task light — on the desk, aimed across the work surface
  2. Bias light — behind the monitor, bouncing off the wall
  3. Ambient light — whatever is already lighting the room

Miss the second one and you get the classic bright-screen-in-a-dark-room fatigue. It is the layer almost everyone skips, and it costs nothing if you already own a lamp.

On a small desk a lamp with a base is a bad trade — it occupies surface you needed. A clamp attaches to the desk edge and uses none.

Two features matter:

Adjustable colour temperature. Neutral or slightly cool during the day, distinctly warm in the evening. This is not a comfort preference; the shift is what stops the desk from feeling like an office at 9pm, and it becomes a useful signal that the working part of the day is over.

An arm that actually reaches. The lamp should be able to sit beside the screen and throw light across the desk, not down onto it from directly above. Light from directly above creates the same shadow problem as the ceiling fixture, just closer.

Aim it across, not down

This is the part people get wrong after buying the right lamp.

Position the head to the side of your working area, angled so the light travels across the desk surface at a shallow angle. Your hands and your paper get lit; your line of sight to the screen does not.

If you can see the bulb or the bright inner face of the shade while looking at the screen, the lamp is aimed wrong. Rotate it until you cannot.

Bias light: the free layer

Put any lamp behind the monitor, aimed at the wall, not at you. A cheap floor lamp works. A small lamp on a shelf works.

What this does is raise the brightness of the area immediately around the screen so your pupils are not constantly adjusting between a bright screen and a dark wall. It is the single biggest reduction in eye strain available for the money, and often the money is zero because you already own the lamp.

Kill the reflection

If the ceiling light reflects off your screen, the fix is usually geometry rather than equipment: rotate the desk a few degrees, or move the monitor forward a few inches.

Matte screens forgive a lot. Glossy screens forgive nothing — with a glossy screen you will have to move something.

Where you cannot move anything, a lower-wattage bulb in the ceiling fixture plus stronger task and bias light produces a better result than fighting the reflection directly.

Two extra lamps means two extra cables, and they arrive after you have already tidied. Clip them along the same route as everything else — one path, not five.

The order to do it in

  1. Bias light behind the monitor — free if you own a lamp, biggest single improvement
  2. Clamp task light, aimed across the desk rather than down
  3. Dim or soften the overhead once the other two are working
  4. Rotate the desk if reflections persist

Most people start at step two and stop there, which is why the desk lamp they bought did not fix the problem. The layer they are missing is the one behind the screen.
