A monitor sits on about ten by eight inches of desk. On a large desk that space is irrelevant. On a desk under four feet wide it is a meaningful fraction of everything you have.

A riser is the cheapest way to get it back, and it fixes a second problem you have probably stopped noticing.

The neck problem you have stopped noticing

Most monitors, on most desks, sit too low. You compensate by tilting your head forward a few degrees. It feels like nothing. Held for eight hours a day it is the most common cause of the end-of-day ache people blame on their chair.

The target is simple: your eyes should land on the top third of the screen when you look straight ahead. Not the middle, not the top edge — the top third.

Sit at your desk normally, close your eyes, then open them looking straight forward. Wherever your gaze lands is your natural eye line. If it hits the bottom half of the screen, the monitor is too low.

Picking the height

Measure the gap between your natural eye line and where the top third currently sits. That number is the riser height you need.

Most people land somewhere between two and five inches. This is why buying a riser "because it looks about right" often disappoints — the correct height is a measurement, not a guess.

Two things matter more than the brand. Depth: it should be slightly deeper than the monitor's base so nothing overhangs. Clearance underneath: enough for whatever you intend to slide under it, which is the entire second reason to buy one.

If you use a laptop rather than a monitor, the same logic applies but the conclusion changes — raising the laptop raises the keyboard too, which is worse. A laptop stand only works alongside a separate keyboard.

What goes underneath

This is where the space comes back. In rough order of usefulness:

The keyboard. Slide it under when you are not typing and the desk becomes a surface again — for reading, for writing by hand, for anything that is not the computer. On a small desk this single habit is the difference between a desk that does one job and a desk that does several.

Flat things you need daily. A notebook, a tablet, the current file. Things that otherwise migrate across the desk all week.

Nothing else. The temptation is to fill it. Resist — the moment it becomes storage for things you do not use daily, the keyboard stops fitting and the main benefit is gone.

The riser handles flat items you use daily. Small loose items — pens, cables, chargers — need somewhere else, and the answer is under the desk rather than on it.

Putting a pen cup on top of the riser undoes the point of the riser. You raised the monitor to free up surface; do not spend that surface on a cup.

Cable slack gets worse, briefly

Raising the monitor adds a few inches of visible cable behind it. On a desk against a wall nobody sees it. On a desk that floats in the room, it is suddenly the most visible thing about your setup.

Clamp-on under-desk cable tray

Route the monitor cable down the back of the riser and into a tray under the desk. Two minutes of work at install time, and you never think about it again.

A note on drawers built into risers

Risers with integrated drawers look efficient and usually are not. The drawers are shallow enough to hold very little, and the unit's footprint ends up larger than the monitor base — so you lose more desk than you gain.

A plain riser plus a separate under-desk drawer costs about the same, holds more, and leaves the keyboard space intact.

Getting it right in one pass

  1. Measure your natural eye line against the screen
  2. Buy for that height, and for a depth greater than the monitor base
  3. Put the keyboard under it, and nothing else
  4. Route the cable down the back into a tray

That is the whole thing. It is a small change and it is the one people who work at a small desk consistently say they should have made sooner.
