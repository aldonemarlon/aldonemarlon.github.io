Standing desk advice assumes you can replace your desk. In a small room that is two problems at once: the new desk costs a few hundred, and getting the old one out of a studio apartment is its own afternoon.

You do not need to replace anything. What you need is a way to change height, and there are three of those that work in a room where the desk is already the largest object.

Option 1: a converter on the desk you have

Desk-top standing converter

Sits on the desk you already own and lifts to standing height, so you get the posture change without buying a second desk or giving up floor space. In a small room this is usually the only version that fits.

A converter sits on your existing desk and lifts the working surface to standing height. It is the answer for most small rooms because it adds zero floor footprint — it uses vertical space, which is the space you have.

Two things to check before buying:

The lowered height. A converter adds a few inches even when down. If your desk is already at a comfortable seated height, a thick converter makes it too tall for sitting, and you have traded one problem for another. Look for one that collapses under about two inches.

The depth. It has to hold the monitor and the keyboard with your forearms supported. A converter shallower than the monitor base is unstable at full height.

Option 2: raise the desk and use a tall chair

This is the cheapest option and almost nobody considers it.

Desk risers — small blocks that go under each leg — lift a whole desk to standing height for very little money. You then work standing, and sit on a tall stool or drafting chair when you want to sit.

It works well if you already lean toward standing most of the day. It works badly if you switch often, because changing means moving a chair rather than moving a surface.

Option 3: a second, higher surface

If you have any shelf, dresser or counter at roughly elbow height, you already own a standing desk. Move the laptop there for an hour or two a day.

This sounds like a compromise and is actually how most people who successfully stand at work end up doing it — the switch takes ten seconds and requires no equipment. The desk stays the desk; the standing happens somewhere else.

Get the height right, whichever you pick

Standing at the wrong height is worse than sitting at the right one.

Elbows at roughly 90 degrees with your forearms parallel to the floor. Screen top at eye level, same rule as sitting — if the converter raises the keyboard but not the screen, you have built a neck problem standing up.

Monitor riser with under-shelf storage

Lifts the screen to eye level and turns the space underneath into storage for a keyboard and notebook. On a desk under 48 inches this is the single change that buys back the most usable surface.

The part everyone skips: the floor

Standing on a hard floor for two hours is worse than sitting for four. An anti-fatigue mat is not optional if you intend to stand for real stretches — and if you have a rug already, put the standing spot on the rug.

The cable problem gets worse

Every height change pulls on every cable. This is the most common reason people stop using a standing setup after two weeks — something unplugs, and re-plugging it becomes part of standing up.

Clamp-on under-desk cable tray

Clamps to the desk edge instead of screwing into it, so it works on glass and on rented furniture. Gets the power strip and the cable slack off the floor, which is what actually makes a small room read as tidy.

What actually works long term

Start with option 3 — the surface you already own — for a week, before spending anything. If you find yourself using it, you now know standing suits you and a converter is worth buying. If you do not, you have learned that for free.
